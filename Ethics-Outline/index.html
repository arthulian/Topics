▾ 1. The Art of Character

  • Ethics is a craft that is learned through practice, not just through reading books.

    • The goal is to shape a good life.

    • Ethics involves developing character through deliberate action.

    • Ancient ethics can be understood as the art of character.

  • The origins of the words ethics and character reflect the idea of a settled disposition.

    • Greek ethos originally meant a stable dwelling and later came to mean stable character.

    • Latin habitus refers to a settled disposition.

    • Excellence develops through deliberate action rather than mere routine.

  • Ancient ethics and modern ethics approach morality differently.

    • Ancient moral thought focused primarily on character.

    • Modern moral codes tend to focus on rules governing actions.

    • Rules alone can create problems:

      • People may look for loopholes.

      • Rules can conflict with one another.

    • The best approach combines the development of good character with respect for good rules.

▾ 2. The Foundations of Ancient Ethics

  • Socrates shifted philosophical attention toward how people should live.

    • He turned away from studying nature and focused on how to live well.

    • He challenged people who pretended to possess wisdom.

    • He described himself as the gadfly stinging Athens awake.

    • He believed that the unexamined life is not worth living.

    • His commitment to these ideas eventually cost him his life.

  • Plato continued Socrates' philosophical tradition.

    • Plato was Socrates' student.

    • He founded the Academy as a rival to Isocrates' school of rhetoric.

    • In the allegory of the cave:

      • Moral education is represented as the soul's journey out of shadows and appearances.

      • The journey leads toward the light of the good.

  • Aristotle viewed ethics as a practical guide for becoming a good person.

    • His Nicomachean Ethics was intended less to teach theory than to make its listeners good.

    • It serves as a field guide in two ways:

      • It develops the character traits necessary for human flourishing.

      • It identifies character types that people should cultivate or avoid.

▾ 3. Responsibility, Choice, and Happiness

  • Responsibility is the starting point for Aristotle's understanding of ethics.

    • Voluntary actions deserve praise or blame.

    • Actions performed under force or in ignorance do not carry the same responsibility.

    • Repeated choices gradually shape a person's character.

    • Greedy and thoughtless habits can make character become "sick."

  • The ultimate goal of ethics is happiness, understood as flourishing.

    • Aristotle uses the concept of eudaimonia.

    • Happiness is not simply the satisfaction of personal preferences.

    • It means flourishing across an entire life.

    • It is pursued for its own sake.

    • It is inseparable from virtue.

  • Human activities can have different kinds of goals.

    • Instrumental activities are performed to achieve an outside result.

    • Autotelic activities are valued for their own sake.

      • Examples include dance and play.

    • MacIntyre illustrates this distinction through a child who is bribed with candy to play chess.

      • The candy represents an external reward.

      • The activity of playing chess can become valuable in itself.

  • Modern ideas echo Aristotle's concept of flourishing.

    • Seligman's elements of well-being.

    • Csikszentmihalyi's concept of flow.

    • Chuang Tzu's idea of effortless ease.

    • The Stoic ideal of a life that flows smoothly.

▾ 4. Virtue: Developing Good Character

  • Virtue is a stable, acquired disposition.

    • Virtue becomes a second nature.

    • It develops through:

      • Imitating good examples.

      • Repeating good actions.

      • Practicing desirable behaviors until they become part of one's character.

  • Virtue involves finding the mean between two extremes.

    • Every virtue represents a balance between too little and too much.

    • A person must avoid both extremes to develop the virtue.

  • The four cardinal virtues are the foundation of moral character.

    • Ambrose identified four "hinge" or cardinal virtues.

    • These virtues echo earlier lists found in Plato and Cicero.

    • The other virtues turn on these four foundational virtues.

4.1 Courage

  • Courage involves mastering the instinct to fight or flee.

    • It balances between:

      • Rashness: too much courage or recklessness.

      • Cowardice: too little courage.

    • Courage underpins every other virtue.

4.2 Moderation

  • Moderation is calm self-mastery over one's appetites.

    • Its Greek name is sophrosyne.

    • It balances between:

      • Self-indulgence.

      • Self-denial.

    • Its opposites include:

      • Grasping greed.

      • Hubris.

4.3 Justice

  • Justice means giving other people what they are due.

    • Its historical imagery includes:

      • The Egyptian goddess Ma'at.

      • The blindfolded figure holding scales and a sword.

    • Justice is the complete virtue when practiced toward others.

    • Its forms include:

      • Commutative justice.

      • Distributive justice.

      • Restorative justice.

      • Political justice.

4.4 Wisdom

  • Wisdom is practical judgment.

    • Greek: phronesis.

    • Latin: prudentia.

    • It involves knowing:

      • The right action.

      • The right time.

      • The right way to act.

  • Wisdom functions as a meta-virtue.

    • It determines which other virtues a particular situation requires.

    • It develops through:

      • Experience.

      • Memory.

      • Humility.

▾ 5. Vices, Sin, and Humility

  • Vices are the opposites of virtues.

  • Dante's understanding of sin is based on love going astray.

    • Lust, gluttony, and greed involve loving too much.

    • Sloth involves loving too little.

    • Wrath, envy, and pride involve loving the wrong things.

    • Pride is considered the worst sin.

  • Humility is the remedy for pride.

    • Humility is the mean between:

      • Pride.

      • Self-abasement.

    • It corrects modern tendencies toward self-inflation.

    • It connects with Aristotle's concept of "greatness of soul."

      • A truly great soul is also humble.

▾ 6. Virtues in Relationships with Other People

  • Social interactions require their own virtues, which also involve finding a mean.

    • Gentleness balances irritability and spinelessness.

    • Truthfulness balances boasting and false modesty.

    • Friendliness balances crankiness and fawning.

    • Wit balances buffoonery and boorishness.

6.1 Friendship

  • Aristotle identifies three kinds of friendship.

    • Friendships based on usefulness.

    • Friendships based on pleasure.

    • Friendships based on shared goodness.

      • These are the rarest and best friendships.

  • Cicero identifies additional qualities that support friendship.

    • Goodwill.

    • Trust.

    • Frankness.

  • Modern relationships raise questions about the nature of friendship.

    • Friendships formed at work.

    • Friendships formed online.

6.2 Civic Virtue and the Good Society

  • A good society depends on virtuous citizens.

    • Citizens need civic virtue.

    • Citizens need a liberal education.

  • World citizenship creates tension with local loyalty.

    • Diogenes and Augustine expressed ideals of world citizenship.

    • These ideals can conflict with loyalty to one's local community.

    • Regardless of whether loyalty is local or global, the classical virtues remain essential.

▾ 7. Natural Law, Human Rights, and Universal Morality

  • Natural law represents the search for universal moral standards.

    • It is understood as an inner moral compass.

    • Its intellectual history runs through:

      • Sophocles.

      • Cicero.

      • Paul.

      • Martin Luther King Jr.

  • People have sometimes used natural law to justify their own customs.

    • Aristotle's defense of slavery is an example.

    • This raises questions about whether something is truly natural or simply reflects the customs of a particular society.

  • The modern concept of human rights developed through several philosophical traditions.

    • Locke's natural rights.

    • Kant's reasoning.

    • The 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights.

  • Moral rights and legal rights are not always the same.

    • A person may have a moral right that is not recognized by law.

    • Reaching agreement about universal rights across cultures remains difficult.

  • The Golden Rule provides a widely shared moral principle.

    • Treat others as you would want to be treated.

    • Some form of this principle appears in nearly every moral tradition.

    • The prohibitive version is called the Silver Rule.

    • Both versions have limitations.

▾ 8. Competing Modern Ethical Theories

8.1 Kantian Ethics: Duty and Universal Rules

  • Kant's ethics focuses on duty.

    • People must respect other persons.

    • People should act only according to rules that could apply universally.

    • This creates difficult moral questions.

      • Example: Is it permissible to lie to a murderer who comes to the door looking for someone?

8.2 Utilitarianism: Consequences and Outcomes

  • Utilitarianism judges actions by their consequences.

    • The goal is to produce the greatest happiness.

  • Bentham's approach emphasizes equal consideration of everyone's pleasure.

    • Everyone's pleasure counts equally.

    • Moral decisions should aim for the greatest overall happiness.

  • Mill distinguishes between different kinds of pleasure.

    • Higher pleasures.

    • Lower pleasures.

8.3 Comparing Ethical Approaches

  • Much everyday moral reasoning is actually cost-benefit thinking.

    • People frequently evaluate decisions based on their perceived costs and benefits.

  • The runaway train dilemma illustrates differences between ethical theories.

    • Different moral approaches can produce different answers to the same situation.

    • The dilemma highlights differences between virtue ethics, duty-based ethics, and utilitarianism.

  • Virtue ethics does not always work well with simplified hypothetical situations.

    • Real moral decisions involve complicated circumstances.

    • Some ethicists combine multiple ethical theories.

    • They distinguish between:

      • What a person intends.

      • Side effects that a person merely foresees.

▾ 9. Care Ethics and Dependence

  • Ethics must account for people who cannot always function as independent adults.

    • Everyone experiences dependence at some point.

    • Examples include:

      • Childhood.

      • Illness.

      • Old age.

  • Care-based ethics recognizes the importance of relationships and dependence.

    • The Lakota ideal of open-hearted reliance illustrates this understanding.

    • Ethical responsibility includes caring for people who depend on others.

▾ 10. Ethics in Medicine

  • The Hippocratic tradition provides four foundational medical ethics principles.

    • Do no harm.

    • Do good.

    • Respect autonomy and informed consent.

    • Act justly.

  • Medical ethics principles can conflict with one another.

    • Example: A patient refuses a blood transfusion.

    • Example: Two patients need the same kidney.

    • These situations create difficult decisions about autonomy, doing good, and justice.

▾ 11. Ethics in the Workplace

  • Ethics applies to every professional role.

    • Ethical questions arise in ordinary workplace behavior as well as serious professional decisions.

    • Examples include:

      • Petty rule-breaking.

      • A police officer facing a situation involving a relative.

      • A company engaging in price gouging.

  • Professional ethics should be consistent with personal ethics.

    • A person should not do something in their professional role that they would refuse to do as an individual.

  • Whistleblowers deserve protection.

    • People who expose unethical conduct should receive protection.

▾ 12. Expanding the Circle of Moral Concern

12.1 Animal Ethics

  • The ethical treatment of animals raises questions about the boundaries of moral concern.

    • Pythagoras advocated vegetarianism.

    • Bentham raised the question of whether animals can suffer.

    • Peter Singer continued this discussion.

  • Animal ethics involves difficult questions.

    • Factory farming.

    • Animal experimentation.

    • Rights for great apes.

12.2 Environmental Ethics

  • Leopold's land ethic expands moral concern to the natural environment.

    • It raises the possibility of legal standing for trees.

    • Ecuador and Bolivia have recognized legal rights of nature.

  • Environmental ethics includes responsibilities to future generations.

    • Moral responsibility extends beyond people living today.

  • Environmental virtue requires the development of several character traits.

    • Humility.

    • Moderation.

    • Wisdom.

    • Courage.

    • A proper orientation toward nature.

▾ 13. The Liberal Arts and the Art of Living Well

  • The liberal arts connect education with the ability to live well.

    • Learning how to learn makes it possible to reflect on an entire life.

    • Education should contribute to the development of character.

  • Every life becomes a self-portrait.

    • A person's life is shaped by the character they choose to develop.

    • The choices people make determine the kind of person they become.

    • Living well is an ongoing process of deliberately crafting one's character.
